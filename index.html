Senate Votes on Energy Permitting and Spending

Public record of one senator set next to the citizen-level mapping approach.

Scope

This page lists verifiable votes and positions by former Senator Joe Manchin (D-WV) on energy permitting, major federal spending packages, and related regulatory questions. It does not assess motives or overall alignment with any party or administration. It only places the record beside the question a voter using the mapping strategy would ask: which viable candidate in a given race is closer to a stated priority on the office that controls it.

Energy permitting and regulatory scope

  • Supported and helped shape permitting-reform language intended to speed approvals for energy infrastructure, including both fossil and renewable projects.
  • Opposed certain regulatory expansions and agency rules that he argued would constrain domestic energy production without corresponding statutory changes.
  • Voted for some energy and climate measures (including portions of the Inflation Reduction Act) while simultaneously pushing to limit the scope of accompanying regulatory provisions.

Under the mapping approach, a voter who ranks faster permitting or limits on new regulatory constraints highly would treat these votes and amendments as directly relevant to the Senate’s power over statutes and appropriations that govern agency behavior.

Major spending packages

  • Voted against or withheld support from several large Democratic spending proposals, citing overall cost and inflation concerns.
  • Supported narrower or revised packages once specific provisions were removed or scaled back.
  • Publicly conditioned support for certain bills on changes to energy, tax, or regulatory language.

A voter who ranks limits on large new spending programs highly would count these positions as evidence about how that Senate seat was used on the relevant appropriations and reconciliation votes.

How the citizen strategy uses this

The mapping method does not require electing any particular senator. It asks, in each race, which of the viable candidates is closer to the voter’s ranked priority on the power that office holds. A senator’s votes on permitting language or spending packages are evidence about those specific powers. The same priorities can be advanced by different candidates in different races and at different levels of government.
